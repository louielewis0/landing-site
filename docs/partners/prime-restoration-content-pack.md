## Post 1

**Title:** The First 24 Hours After Water Damage: A Michigan Homeowner's Checklist
**Slug:** /first-24-hours-water-damage-checklist
**Meta description:** What to do — and not do — in the first day after a burst pipe, backup, or flood in your Michigan home, from a licensed Sterling Heights restoration company. 24/7 line: (586) 277-1069.

**The short answer (put this first):** Stop the water, kill power to affected
areas if safe, document everything for insurance before you move it, and get
drying started within the first day — mold risk and repair scope both grow
with every hour materials stay wet. Call a licensed restoration company
before you call a general handyman; mitigation speed is the whole game.

**Body outline:**
1. **Stop the source.** Main shutoff location (tell readers to find theirs
   NOW, before an emergency). Supply-line vs drain vs groundwater — why the
   source changes the cleanup class.
2. **Safety before salvage.** Electricity + standing water; sewage backups
   are a health hazard, not a mop job.
3. **Document before you touch.** Photos/video of everything, keep damaged
   materials until the adjuster says otherwise, start a written timeline.
4. **Call your insurer AND a restoration contractor the same day.** What a
   professional mitigation visit actually does (extraction, moisture
   mapping, drying equipment) and why DIY fans-and-towels under-dries
   wall cavities.
5. **What NOT to do.** Don't run home HVAC to "dry it out" after sewage or
   mold-suspect events; don't paint over stains before drying is verified;
   don't sign an assignment-of-benefits you haven't read.
6. **CTA:** 24/7 line (586) 277-1069 · free estimate · licensed & insured
   (MI Builder License #262500491).
7. **FAQ (with FAQPage schema):** How fast can mold start? (general answer:
   within days on wet materials — say "commonly cited as 24–72 hours" only
   if citing a source like EPA/IICRC, else keep qualitative.) Does insurance
   cover it? Should I keep receipts?

## Post 2

**Title:** Water Damage and Selling Your Home in Michigan: What You Must Disclose (and What Buyers Actually Care About)
**Slug:** /water-damage-selling-disclosure-michigan
**Meta description:** Michigan's Seller Disclosure Act requires you to disclose known water damage. Here's how a documented, professional repair protects your sale price — and what buyers' inspectors look for.

**The short answer:** Michigan's Seller Disclosure Act (Act 92 of 1993,
MCL 565.951–966) requires sellers to deliver a written statement of the
property's known condition — known water damage and the repairs belong on
it. The good news: buyers discount *undocumented* damage far more than
professionally remediated damage. A licensed contractor's invoice with a
clear scope turns a red flag into a closed item.

**Body outline:**
1. What the disclosure form covers (link the statute at legislature.mi.gov).
2. Why concealment backfires (inspection, post-closing liability).
3. The paperwork that protects your price: mitigation report, moisture
   readings, invoice, warranty.
4. When restoration-before-listing pays vs when as-is makes sense — and
   link here to REMC's guide:
   https://marketcenterrealty.com/water-damage-sell-or-restore
   ("Our real-estate partner Real Estate Market Center wrote the seller's
   side of this decision") — the reciprocal link is natural here because
   the topics genuinely interlock.

## Post 3

**Title:** Mold After Water Damage: When It's a Wipe-Down and When It's a Remediation Job (Michigan Guide)
**Slug:** /mold-after-water-damage-michigan
**Meta description:** Not all mold requires professional remediation — but hidden moisture usually does. An IICRC-protocol Sterling Heights company on where the line actually is.

**The short answer:** Small surface mold on non-porous materials with the
moisture source fixed is often a homeowner-level cleanup. It becomes a
professional remediation job when mold follows a water event you didn't
fully dry, appears on porous materials (drywall, insulation, subfloor),
covers a large area, keeps returning, or anyone in the house has
respiratory issues. The dividing line is almost always hidden moisture —
what you can see is rarely the whole colony.

**Body outline:**
1. Why Michigan basements are the repeat offender (freeze-thaw, spring
   water tables, aging clay laterals — qualitative, no invented stats).
2. The visible/hidden distinction; why "it came back after bleach" means
   moisture, not cleaning technique.
3. What professional remediation actually involves (containment, negative
   air, HEPA, verification) — plain-language, per IICRC protocol as
   referenced on the site.
4. Health honesty: don't diagnose; advise sensitive individuals to keep
   distance and consult professionals.
5. CTA: inspection/estimate, 24/7 line.
